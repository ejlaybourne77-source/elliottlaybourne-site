Extreme Fear in the Stock Market: Buy the Dip or Wait?

Seven straight down days, Nvidia down 14% in a day and the VIX near 36. Here is the machine behind it, and the one thing to watch before you buy back in.

22 November 2026 · Elliott Laybourne · 5 min read
In short
  • Last week's selloff was mostly mechanical: put buying forced market makers to sell, and options expiry made it worse.
  • The biggest up days happen in stressed markets, and most are followed by a down day.
  • Watch for the VIX to come down and stay down before you buy back in.

Seven straight down days for the S&P 500, with the market down almost 9.5% in that time. On Friday, Nvidia fell 14% in a single day. If you're confused about how to read this market, that's normal. And if your first thought is "how do I make money from this volatility?", I'd stop you there. Without the right tools for the job, it's very hard to make money in conditions like these and very easy to lose it.

I spent twenty years as a derivatives specialist, market maker and prop trader. What I can tell you is that a lot of what you saw last week wasn't panic. It was mechanical. Once you see the machine, the moves start to make sense.

How the crowded trade cracked

For most of the last two years, a handful of big AI stocks carried the whole market higher. When a trade that crowded starts to break, the selling rarely stays in one stock. The big funds exposed to it have to reduce risk elsewhere, and that is how stress moves out of a couple of big names and into the wider market.

The insurance loop

Put options are insurance against the market falling. When you buy a put, the market maker who sells it to you has to hedge their own risk, and they do that by going short S&P 500 futures.

In a panic, lots of people rush to buy puts at the same time, so market makers have to short a lot of futures. That selling pushes the S&P down. A lower market makes people buy more puts, which means more hedging, more selling and higher implied volatility. You end up with a feedback loop that can push the market down very, very quickly.

Why it sped up into Friday

Friday was the monthly options expiration, which falls on the third Friday of every month. Monthly expiries carry a huge amount of open interest, and more options means more hedging. As expiration approaches, puts become very sensitive, so with every dollar the S&P falls, market makers are forced to sell more futures.

That is why Friday was the worst day of the run. The S&P fell 3.7%, the Nasdaq 100 fell 5.6%, the semiconductor index fell more than 10%, and Nvidia fell 14%. The VIX, the market's implied volatility index, closed at almost 36.

The twist

A lot of those options expired on Friday, and the ones left lose a little value every day, including weekends when the market is shut. As they decay and volatility comes down, they decay even faster. Now the same machine runs in reverse: the market makers who were forced to sell all the way down can be forced to buy on the way back up. That is often why a volatility spike ends in a V-shaped recovery.

The big green day trap

After a spike like this, a big up move over the next few days is common. In fact, all 20 of the best S&P days since 2014 came when the VIX was above 20. The biggest up days happen when the market is stressed.

But a big up day is not the all clear. In 13 of those 20, the market fell the next day. In March 2020, the S&P jumped 9.3% in a day and fell 12% the following day. On 9 April 2025, it rose more than 9.5%, and the next day's low was almost 7% below that day's high. A big green candle is just what a stressed market looks like when it is volatile in both directions.

Why buying a high VIX can wipe you out

You'll hear that the best time to buy stocks is when the VIX is high. It's half right. The VIX does mean revert. Since 2014, it closed above 35 nine times before this Friday, and a month later it was lower in eight of them.

The problem is the ninth. In February 2020, the VIX closed above 35 and the S&P went on to fall another 24.9% in under a month. Buying a high VIX works most of the time, but the time it doesn't can wipe you out, and you never know in advance which one you're trading.

Watch the volatility, not the candles

So instead of watching the candles, watch implied volatility. You're not just waiting for the VIX to come down. You want it to come down and stay down for a few days. The big funds that are forced to sell when volatility rises can't come back in until volatility has stayed low for a while.

A calm VIX doesn't promise a full recovery or a return to the highs. It says conditions are safe for now. Every day it says that, more of the money that was forced out is likely to be pulled back in, because nobody wants a drawdown, but nobody wants to be left on the sidelines either. Your job isn't to catch the bottom. It's to get back in when implied volatility says it's safer to do so.

Fight or flight

When markets get like this, your brain gives you two options: fight or flight. You'll probably want to fight. Flight is the safe choice. Run away from trouble, not towards it, wait for volatility to calm, then start moving back in.

What to watch next week

Implied volatility is stretched, and options get two weekend days to decay. Next week is a short Thanksgiving week, three and a half trading days followed by another two days with the market closed, which means plenty more decay. That points to market makers being in buy mode, and there is room for a fast snapback.

It all depends on Monday. With that buying behind it, the market should get off to a good start. A good start could carry stocks higher through the week. A bad start would suggest someone big really wants out, and that something is really wrong.

The Nasdaq rose 7% on Monday. That's the good start this post said to watch for, with options decaying over the weekend and market makers in buy mode. A good start could carry stocks higher through the week, but it isn't an all clear. The test is whether the VIX comes down and stays down.
